# Section: Introduction

The introduction is the paper's packaging and its roadmap: background and brief history, what others (and the authors) have done, the motivation, and the main results — a few hundred words, complete but not bloated. Good journals weight it heavily.

## The four whys a good introduction answers

1. Why now? (timeliness)
2. Why this problem? (the challenge)
3. Why this method? (its advantage)
4. Why should the reader care? (novelty, better results, new findings, new tools)

## Opening (first paragraph — the most important real estate)

- **Never open with a dump of symbols and definitions.** Start with a plain-language description of the problem that any member of the broad community can follow; symbols arrive when needed.
- **Apply Higham's delete-the-first-sentence test.** If the first sentence states common knowledge ("Polynomials are widely used as approximating functions ..."), delete it and start with the actual problem. Chinese-authored drafts are especially prone to opening with self-evident background and claims of broad significance — cut them.
- Go straight to the problem. A short historical anchor is fine when it sets up the question (three sentences from Waring 1770 to the paper's actual question).
- If simple, self-evident notation genuinely eases later reading, a small amount may appear early — the exception, not the rule.

## Middle

Typically covers, in any order:

1. define the problem;
2. explain what the paper attempts;
3. summarize prior main results and their shortcomings — cite the most relevant literature (this shows the work is not a redundancy);
4. outline the plan of attack.

State the motivation explicitly: a gap ("To fill this gap, we present ..."), an inadequacy of existing approaches, a simpler or more effective route. Review only work directly related to this paper, especially results and methods used later — not the field's whole history.

## Ending

Unless the paper is short or has a table of contents, close with an outline paragraph:

- open with "This paper is organized as follows" / "An outline of this paper is as follows";
- give each later section one sentence describing what it does — do not list section titles, and do not repeat their titles verbatim.

## Tone

- Objective and measured. No self-praise ("Our new result is extremely important ..."), no referee-style attacks ("the main result of [3] is trivial", "[3] is completely wrong"). Even when pointing out a genuine error, do it gently.
- Never transplant sentences verbatim from others' papers or the authors' own earlier papers. Quoted sentences need quotation marks and citation.

Stock phrases for openings and discussion ("attracted considerable attention", "The main purpose of this paper is to ...", "There are two main motivations ...") are in `references/math-phrasebook.md`.
