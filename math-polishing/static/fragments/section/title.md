# Section: Title

The title is the paper's face and its advertisement: for every person who reads the full text, hundreds read only the title.

## The job

A title must, in the fewest words:

1. say what the paper does and what its main contribution is;
2. carry searchable keywords so the paper can be found;
3. let a reader judge whether to read the abstract, and signal the paper type (survey / theoretical / applied / computational);
4. differ from existing titles.

## Rules

- **Specific, not generic.** "Computational methods for Euler equations" or "Numerical analysis for fluid mechanics" are book titles, not paper titles — the reader learns nothing about the contribution.
- **Delete empty openers:** "Studies on ...", "Investigations on ...", "Observations on ...". They carry no information.
- **Name the contribution.** Good shapes: "An algorithm for the machine calculation of complex Fourier series"; "Viscosity solutions of Hamilton–Jacobi equations"; "A proof of Minkowski's inequality for convex curves".
- **Question titles** ("Can one hear the shape of a drum?", "How long is the coast of Britain?") work for expository or problem-posing papers; use sparingly for research papers.
- **Narrow crowded fields with qualifiers.** When the area is mature, add the distinguishing words: "Generation of finite difference formulas on arbitrarily spaced grids" — "arbitrarily" is the load-bearing word.
- **Gerund + using** is an acceptable shape: "Overcoming corner singularities using multigrid methods".
- **No logically redundant modifier stacks.** "A weakly and strongly convergent algorithm" is flawed on its face (strong implies weak); pick the stronger or just "convergent".
- **Not too long, not too short.** Long titles usually carry dead words at the front; very short ones ("Yet another chaotic attractor") work only when the result genuinely speaks for itself.
- Respect field conventions — pure math, applied math, and engineering title habits differ. Check comparable papers in the target journal before finalizing.
